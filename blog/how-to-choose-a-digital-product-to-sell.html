How to Choose a Digital Product to Sell (Without Guessing)

MPM Studio · ~4 min read

Choosing a digital product to sell feels harder than making one. Endless ideas, no clear signal, and the fear of building something nobody buys. The fix isn't more ideas — it's a filter. Here is the same four-step filter we use to pick every product before we put it on the shelf.

Step 1: Sell the outcome, not the file

The most common mistake is starting with a file. "I could make a PDF." "I could record a course." Start with what the buyer wants after the purchase, then work backwards to the file.

  • The outcome is the change the buyer experiences — landing a client, finishing a project, fixing a process.
  • The file is just the container that delivers that change.

Ask: "What does this person want to be done?" Then ask: "What is the smallest file that gets them there?" If two files deliver the same outcome, sell the simpler one — simpler ships faster and generates fewer support questions.

This is the outcome-over-file rule. Every product decision below flows from it.

Step 2: Pick one of the four faceless audiences

Digital products work without a personal brand when the buyer relates to a situation, not a personality. Four archetypes cover most of the market:

ArchetypeWhat they wantExample product
Micro-business operatorsSave time, standardize workInvoice templates, SOP packs, pricing guides
Niche hobbyistsDo their hobby better or fasterSkill checklists, gear guides, project planners
Career switchers & side-hustlersProve competence, get startedInterview prep, portfolio prompts, starter plans
Late-night-googlersSolve a problem right nowOne-page fixes, walkthroughs, decision guides

Write a one-line description of your buyer from one of these rows: who they are, when they reach for your product, and what they want to be true afterwards. If you can't write that line, the product isn't chosen yet.

Step 3: Apply the pain-urgency + buy-in-placement filter

Two questions decide whether the product will sell:

Pain + urgency. Is the problem painful enough that someone would pay to remove it this week? Urgency comes from a deadline or a live cost — a lost client, a stuck project, a missed opportunity.

Buy-in placement. Where does the buyer already reach into their wallet? Products that sit next to money already being spent convert better. A template that keeps a freelancer's project on track slides into an existing workflow with an existing budget. A product that asks the buyer to invent a new habit or a new budget has to create its own buy-in.

The best beginner products sit at the intersection: high pain-urgency and close to existing spending. If you only hit one, keep looking.

Step 4: Validate in five to seven days

You don't need a finished product to test demand. A five-to-seven-day validation looks like this:

  • Day 1–2. Write the sales page for the outcome, not the file. List the transformation the buyer gets.
  • Day 3–4. Put a buy button on it and drive a small amount of traffic — one community, one Pinterest pin board, one email to people who already follow you.
  • Day 5–7. Watch what happens. A few sales or several strong waitlist sign-ups means build it fully. Silence of click-throughs but no intent means adjust the offer before you build a thing.

Validation measures intent, not sales volume. One buyer after 100 targeted visits tells you more than a thousand ad impressions.

How to format it

Once a concept passes the filter, match the format to the outcome:

Outcome wantedBest format
Do a task step-by-stepChecklist, walkthrough, video
Fill in the blanksTemplates, planners, swipe files
Decide between optionsComparison guide, decision matrix
Learn a set of skillsMini-course, practice prompts
Avoid a repeat mistakeJob aids, error checklists, SOP

The format follows the job. When in doubt, pick the format the buyer can use without you.

Choosing is a decision, then a test

Four steps, no guesswork: lead with the outcome, place the buyer in one of the four archetypes, run the pain-urgency and buy-in-placement filter, and validate in a week. A product that clears all four is worth building — and building it is its own fast, separate job.
